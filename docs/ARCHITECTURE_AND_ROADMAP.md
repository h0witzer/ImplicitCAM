# ImplicitCAM — Architecture & Roadmap

**A functional-representation (F-rep) CAM kernel for subtractive machining.**
Toolpaths are generated by *walking scalar fields*, not by combinatorial B-rep/mesh geometry.
Target: high-efficiency 2.5D roughing first, building on a framework that reaches
**simultaneous 5-axis** machining as the intended endpoint.

Status: design baseline (pre-implementation).
See also: `ROUGHING_FEASIBILITY.md` (2026-09-28) - feasibility of replacing offset roughing;
proposes changes to sections 2, 6, 8, 9 and Appendix A (its section 10).
License: AGPL-3.0-only (see `LICENSE`); commercial licenses for closed products and services from the copyright holder.
Priority (2026-09-29): generator work is ranked field-native first - methods that use what only
fields can do (queries anywhere, free topology, solved plans, gradients, state over time,
continuity) and are measurably different from the incumbents. The test and the ranking are in
`ROUGHING_FEASIBILITY.md` section 13.

## Part I — Foundational Thesis & Decisions

This section is the explicit record of the reasoning behind the project. It exists so that
future decisions can be checked against *why* the architecture is shaped this way.

### 1. The core thesis

Conventional CAM generates toolpaths as **combinatorial geometry**: intersect trimmed
NURBS surfaces, offset boundary loops, trim/stitch curves, project points onto
tessellations. This is what makes B-rep CAM fragile (offset/blend/boolean failures) and
what every commercial high-efficiency-machining (HEM) patent is written in the language of
("parallel offsets," "transition paths bridging offsets," "morphing spirals matching
boundaries").

ImplicitCAM generates toolpaths as **field processing**: the part, the stock, and the
toolpath itself are continuous scalar fields over ℝ³, and paths *emerge* as the solution of
a PDE, the streamline of a gradient, or the trajectory of a constrained integration through
a field. This is a categorically different *mechanism*, which is the source of both its
robustness advantages and its patent-distinctness.

### 2. What we are explicitly NOT doing

- **Not a voxel/mesh CAM.** We reject the "cache the SDF to a uniform grid and mesh it"
  shortcut. (See the cautionary case study in §4.)
- **Not trusting field *magnitude* where it is unreliable.** See the Euclidean-distance
  crux in §5.
- **Not building offset-shelling roughing.** The roughing strategy walks the field with
  closed-loop engagement feedback; it does not construct concentric offset passes. (§7)
  The closed-loop engagement walk is itself superseded by the next item.
- **Not using the incumbents' toolpath constructions as our template (Derek, 2026-10-23,
  revised 2026-10-26; decision 8 in `CONTRIBUTING.md`).** Engagement-controlled roughing as
  commercial CAM does it is a set of patented methods for a physics objective: keep tool load,
  chip thickness and engagement inside what the tool and machine can take. The patents protect
  the methods, not the objective; the objective is ours too, pursued through the plan field
  with chip thickness as the load measure and feed free in the plan (engagement is measured,
  never the target). Ruled out as methods, in any form including fallbacks, in-repo baselines,
  flags, tests and plans: offset or contour-parallel passes; engagement held by offsetting,
  trimming or searching boundary curves, offset rings or cleared-area polygons; engagement
  held by steering a walker step by step until the measured cut matches a target; spirals
  morphed or interpolated between boundary curves; transition or bridge moves built between
  offset passes; recovering a boundary curve from a field and then doing any of these. Results
  may converge; intent may not (an incumbent construction is never the stated target). The
  field approach should differ most where the classic constructions fail (§10). Trochoids and
  field spirals were taken off the list on 2026-10-26 because prior art clears them; they stay
  clear of the live claims in §10.

- **YouTube: "I'm making a game engine based on dynamic SDFs"** (holger-kibur). Assessed

  interpolates, and **meshes** the result for a normal rasterizer; carving = instantiating

### 4. Case study: why the "dynamic SDF" game engine is a cautionary tale, not a template

The engine exhibits exactly the failure modes ImplicitCAM must avoid:

- **CSG-tree explosion** — carving by instantiating primitives makes evaluation cost grow
  with edit history ("number of instances is going to skyrocket"). → Drives our *stock*
  representation decision (§6).
- **Clay-like blending** — interpolated grid sampling produces smooth merges; this is the
  field no longer being a true distance field, made visible. → Drives the Euclidean crux
  (§5).
- **Meshing reintroduces faceting** — the chordal error we are trying to escape.

The lesson: pure analytic evaluation per-frame is too expensive for a real-time game on a
consumer GPU, so the dev took the voxel shortcut. CAM does not have that excuse for the
*part*, but it *does* legitimately want a discretized *stock* (§6) — a different object with
different requirements.

### 5. The Euclidean-distance crux (the single most important technical fact)

An F-rep kernel gives a function `f(x)` that is:

- **Exact in sign** (inside/outside) — reliable.
- **Lipschitz-1** (`|∇f| ≤ 1`) if built carefully — reliable.
- **NOT the true Euclidean distance** in general. The moment you compose with `min` (union)
  / `max` (intersection), the result is a *bound*, not a distance.

The error is **structured and localizable**: it concentrates at **concave junctions** and
near the **medial axis**; elsewhere the field is essentially exact. R-functions (Rvachev /
Pasko–Shapiro F-rep) give smooth booleans and clean derivatives but **do not** restore
Euclidean distance — they solve differentiability, not the metric.

**Consequences by use case:**

| Need | Requirement | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| Inside/outside test | Correct sign (have it) | free |
| **Conservative gouge/collision safety** | A *lower bound* on clearance — the Lipschitz field is *ideal* (underestimating clearance is safe) | free |
| Cutter drop / contact (3-axis, waterline) | *Directed* distance along a ray | interval root-find, **no grid** |
| Surface normal | `∇f/|∇f|` from the bound | free |
| **Precision offsets (levels)** | True Euclidean distance in a band | needs redistancing |
| **Shells (uniform wall)** | True Euclidean distance both sides | needs redistancing |

**Two design rules fall out of this table:**

- **RULE — Two fields, not one.** Maintain a cheap *conservative Lipschitz* field for
  safety-critical clearance/collision (where erring toward "closer than reality" is safe),
  and an expensive *exact-in-a-band* field only where precision boundaries are computed.
- **RULE — A large class of operations sidesteps exact distance entirely** via *directed*
  distance (interval root-finding on the raw analytic field), which needs no grid.

**Exact-distance recovery, when genuinely needed (finishing, shells):**

- **Per-query closest-point projection** (grid-free): Newton-iterate
  `x ← x − f(x)·∇f(x)/|∇f|²` onto the zero-set; distance to the converged point is
  near-exact. Fails at the medial axis — but that locus is *also* where offsets
  self-intersect and shells collide with themselves, so detecting it is simultaneously the
  accuracy guard and the "feature too small / wall too thin" detector.
- **Adaptive narrow-band redistancing**: solve the eikonal `|∇d| = 1` only in the band
  around the surface, seeded from closest-point distances (keeps the zero-set exact).
  Solvers: Fast Marching (O(N log N)), **Fast Sweeping** (O(N), parallelizable — preferred),
  Fast Iterative (GPU). *Heat method smooths the medial axis → wrong for sharp offsets.*
  Prefer delegating this to **OpenVDB** (production-grade FastSweeping) rather than building
  from scratch.

**This machinery is a FINISHING concern and is deferred (Phase 2+). Roughing does not need
it — see §7.**

### 6. Stock vs part: the one place discretization is correct

- **Part / design → analytic F-rep tree** (Fidget). Infinite precision where it matters
  (finishing).
- **In-process stock → discretized narrow-band field** (tri-dexel, or a VDB-style sparse
  SDF). The stock is `S ← S ∧ ¬(swept tool volume)` accumulated over thousands of
  subtractions; kept as an analytic CSG tree it explodes (the §4 failure). The stock is a
  *transient bookkeeping object at roughing tolerance* (0.1–0.5 mm), whose real needs are
  fast incremental boolean subtraction and fast local queries. That is exactly what dexels
  and OpenVDB are built for.

**Analytic part, discretized stock — chosen per object, on purpose.** This resolves the
central tension: we stay implicit where precision matters and discretize only a transient,
loose-tolerance accumulator.

### 7. Why field-walking roughing is robust to the Euclidean crux

"Dumb offset shelling" *trusts field magnitude* ("the r-level-set is my next pass"), so it
needs exact EDFs. **Field-walking does not:**

1. **Direction survives the bound.** `∇f/|∇f|` points at the nearest wall correctly even
   when `|∇f|` is wrong. Walking consumes *direction* (preserved), not *distance*
   (corrupted).
2. **Arc length is measured, not read off the field.** Path length comes from the
   integrator, so metric error never enters it.
3. **Engagement is measured locally, not inferred from offsets.** Radial engagement = the
   angular arc of the tool's cutting circle still inside the *live stock*, obtained by
   sampling the stock field around the tool at the actual position.

**RULE — Derive stepover/pitch from *measured* engagement via feedback, never from field
values.** This turns roughing into a closed loop on a quantity we can measure, which is
*why* field-walking beats offset shelling and not merely differs from it: offset shelling is
open-loop on an untrustworthy metric; field-walking is closed-loop on ground truth.

**Caveat — the one place the crux *does* re-enter: the `δ − P` boundary.** Points 1–3 concern
the *walk*. But the removable region's part-side boundary is the level set `{P = δ}` (§8),
which reads `P`'s *magnitude* at the δ threshold. Since `P` is a Lipschitz *lower bound* on
true distance (§5), `{P = δ}` lies at true distance `≥ δ` from the part — so actual finish
stock is **non-uniform and bulges inward at concave junctions and near the medial axis**,
exactly where the bound is loosest. This never gouges (leaving *extra* stock is safe), so
roughing is unaffected; but it hands **variable stock to the finisher**, worst at the concave
features where constant-engagement finishing is hardest. The excess is bounded by the bound's
looseness *at the δ-band*, and δ is small — so it is small except at sharp concavities.
**This is the specific quantity the Phase-0 error-budget experiment must measure: offset error
in the δ-band at concave features, not generic offset error.** (Likewise, "ground truth" above
is the *discretized* stock model — its resolution caps engagement-measurement precision, so
resolve the dexel finely enough where the controller's tolerance demands it.)

### 8. The field-walking HEM algorithm (baseline formulation)

A field-driven morphing spiral; no offset curves are ever constructed.

**Removable region** — with part field `P`, live stock `S`, stock-to-leave `δ`:
```
R(x) = max( S(x),  δ − P(x) )      →  removable where R < 0
```
(inside stock AND outside part-plus-leave). One field expression built from the two fields
we already maintain.

**The walk** — the trajectory is a flow on a 2D *leaf* of a foliation of `{R<0}`. Within a
leaf, compute a wall-distance and at the tool position blend:
- **tangential** `t̂` ⟂ `∇R` *restricted to the leaf tangent plane* (follow the wall — one
  spiral loop),
- **inward** `n̂` down the wall-distance gradient (toward the region's medial spine).

Integrate `v = normalize(t̂ + k·n̂)` (RK4). The trajectory *is* a morphing spiral that hugs
the boundary and spirals inward — emergent from vector-field integration.

**Why "on a leaf" is the load-bearing phrasing.** `t̂ ⟂ ∇R` is a *direction* only when the
tangent space is 1-D. In ℝ³, `⟂∇R` is a 2-plane, so the walk direction is underdetermined
until a foliation reduces each locus to a 2D surface — and slicing supplies that foliation
*implicitly*. Stating it explicitly is what keeps the walk from being secretly 2.5D:
- *3-axis restriction (MVP):* foliation = horizontal planes `{z = const}`; leaves = Z-slabs;
  `⟂∇R` within a leaf is a single tangential sense. This is exactly the per-slab walk below.
- *5-axis general:* foliation = part-adapted surfaces (e.g. offset surfaces of `P`, or
  drive/flow-guide surfaces), with the tool axis `â` slaved to — or optimized around — the
  leaf normal. The blend *within a leaf* is identical; only the foliation choice and the
  axis-coupling generalize. **Open (Phase 4):** how the foliation is chosen for a general
  part, and how tightly `â` is coupled to the leaf normal, are research items — but the
  walk's contract ("flow on a leaf of a foliation") is dimension-general and must be written
  that way now, not discovered when the slab is removed.

*Superseded (Derek, 2026-10-23): the step-by-step engagement servo below is an incumbent
method, ruled out by §2 (decision 8 in `CONTRIBUTING.md`) and by the field-native test
(`ROUGHING_FEASIBILITY.md` section 13.2: nothing is discovered by walking). Trochoids were
allowed again on 2026-10-26, but not as the servo's output: injecting one when the measured
engagement passes a target is still the servo. The objective it serves, holding the cut inside
physical limits, stays, pursued through the plan field. Kept as a record; do not implement it.*

**Engagement feedback** — each step, sample `S` around the cutting circle for the true
engaged arc `θ`; servo pitch `k`:
- `θ > θ_target` → shrink `k`, or inject a trochoidal loop to shed engagement;
- `θ < θ_target` → grow `k`.
Trochoids **emerge** from the controller; they are not scripted.

**Stock update + guaranteed coverage** — subtract the swept tool volume from `S`; then
query `{R<0}`: any remaining component is uncut rest-material → re-seed a walker there.
Seeds go at local minima of wall-distance (medial points of the removable region).

**2.5D / 3-axis specialization** — the horizontal-slab foliation: per Z-slab at high axial depth (deep axial, low radial,
constant engagement = the "high efficiency"). The **2D medial axis of the removable region
per slab** (exact via OpenVoronoi) supplies seeds and the spiral spine.

### 9. Kernel & stack decision

- **Field core → Fidget** (Rust). Chosen for the *evaluation hot loop*: JIT + SIMD
  point/interval/gradient modes are exactly what toolpath generation and redistancing
  hammer. Interval arithmetic is a *correctness* primitive (provably safe cell pruning →
  gouge-free guarantees). Rust is a better foundation for a new numerically-heavy codebase.
  Caveat: API is young ("Lego-kit"); mitigated because our IP is our own code atop the
  *stable* eval primitives, and Rust vendoring/forking is painless.
- **Meshing / visualization → libfive** (fallback) or direct GPU raymarching of the field.
  libfive's watertight/manifold/feature-preserving mesher is more battle-tested than
  Fidget's, but meshing is peripheral to an implicit-native design, so this is optional.
- **Live stock → tri-dexel (native Rust) for 2.5D/3-axis**, migrating to a VDB-style sparse
  narrow-band SDF (OpenVDB via FFI, or a Rust reimplementation) for true 3D/5-axis.
- **2D medial axis → OpenVoronoi** (exact, C++ via FFI) for slab seeds/spine; a grid
  skeleton from the stock field is an acceptable Phase-1 stand-in.
- **Narrow-band redistancing (Phase 2+) → OpenVDB FastSweeping.**

*Neither kernel solves the Euclidean crux — that is our algorithm layer's job (§5).*

**Amendment 2026-09-29 (Derek): GPU-native exact kernels over an F-rep core.** Supersedes
the Fidget bullet above and two parked items in Appendix A (GPU evaluation, B-rep ingestion).
*Revised the same day:* the first version made exact B-rep faces the internal
representation, which quietly dropped the free conservative gouge guarantee (section 5
table: a Lipschitz lower bound on clearance plus interval pruning). The project is
architected to take maximum advantage of implicit geometry and F-rep; B-rep is an *import
format*, not the core.

- **F-rep is the only internal representation.** Every stage after import - planning,
  gouge and collision checks, engagement, medial axis, reach, certification - reads fields,
  never faces. Three importers produce the same F-rep: **B-rep** (the primary source for
  shop parts), **procedural F-rep / DSL** (textures, lattices, test shapes), and **mesh**
  (only when the mesh *is* the input: a scan, an STL-only file). An exact input is never
  tessellated first; an earlier demo's triangle-shadow clearance is the anti-pattern
  this rules out.
- **B-rep import = compile to one global F-rep, pruned per cell.** *(Rewritten 2026-10-27,
  Derek: no CSG tree is compiled; the field is the exact signed distance itself. See the last
  entry of this section.)* Every B-rep solid is
  exactly an F-rep (its signed distance to its own boundary); import makes that explicit:
  - leaves are exact face primitives (planes, cylinders, cones, spheres, tori, NURBS) with
    their trim loops, each tagged with its source face id;
  - faces meeting at a convex edge combine with `max`, at a concave edge with `min`. The
    B-rep topology decides the operator, not a winding count;
  - a spatial grid holds, per cell, the pruned tree of every face that can be nearest
    anywhere in that cell. The pruned tree is a *restriction of the one global function*,
    never a separate local function, so neighboring cells agree on their shared boundary
    and the field has no seams (a seam kinks the gradient walk and fails the `continuity`
    gate). This is Fidget-style tape pruning applied to faces.
- **Two fields from the same cell data** (the two-field discipline of section 5):
  - *bound field*: `min`/`max` of the untrimmed surface fields. A Lipschitz lower bound on
    clearance with cheap interval evaluation - gouge and collision checks, cell pruning,
    interval-over-time certification (Advantage 6). Loose outside convex edges, which only
    leaves extra stock (safe).
  - *exact field*: unsigned distance to the trimmed faces, signed from the topology. True
    Euclidean distance, edges included - finish offsets, the `delta - P` boundary (section
    7 caveat), scallop.
  - NURBS leaves carry a certified interval bound from their control hull (Bezier pieces).
    The iterative closest-point solve supplies the exact value, never the bound.
- **Tools are exact too:** any profile of lines and arcs revolved about the axis (flat,
  ball, bull, V, chamfer, tapered, engraver), with shank and holder as more profile
  segments, so holder collision is the same math.
- **One core query, one contact record.** Tool at a pose against the part field returns
  the signed clearance (or, for a vertical tool, the lowest gouge-free tip height), its
  certified lower bound from the bound field, the contact face id (from the leaf tag) and
  tool segment, the contact point, gradient and curvature, and the second-nearest contact.
  Planning, scallop, medial axis, reach and certification read the record; 2.5D, 3-axis
  surfacing and later 5-axis are the same query.
- **Custom GPU kernels in Rust + wgpu (WGSL)**, not an interpreted expression tape: each
  cell's pruned tree is compiled to straight-line WGSL over its leaf data. Fidget's GPU
  branch is experimental and its author calls the GPU interpreter loop "incredibly
  inefficient"; Prospero's fastest GPU entries compiled the expression to a shader instead.
  Every importer's output gets the same treatment. Fidget stays in the CPU workspace as an
  independent check of the bound field on trees whose leaves it can express (the analytic
  primitives), not as the production evaluator.
- **Precision.** WGSL has no double type, so kernels run single precision in local frames
  with rounding folded into the certification margin; an exact double-precision CPU
  implementation of every query lives in the same workspace, every GPU kernel is tested
  against it, and borderline gouge verdicts are re-decided on the CPU. icam-stock stays
  the independent end-to-end checker.
- **GPU first (2026-10-24, Derek; decision 9 in `CONTRIBUTING.md`).** Field queries are
  independent per point, cell, pixel or pose, so planning, checking and visualization are
  meant to be embarrassingly parallel. Every loop over samples, cells, poses or pixels is
  considered for a GPU kernel first, and code that stays on the CPU says why. Formulations
  are chosen to parallelize (fast sweeping or the fast iterative method over fast marching,
  Jacobi or multigrid over Gauss-Seidel, per-point walk-on-spheres); CPU work that remains
  uses `rayon`, and fields are visualized in shaders. The double-precision CPU reference
  above stays the check on every kernel.
- **Input hygiene is out of scope.** Leaky or inconsistent STEP shells go to a
  preprocessor ahead of the importer when they become a real problem; until then the
  importer assumes a closed, consistent shell.
- **Code:** `crates/` in this repository (Cargo workspace). The reference queries in
  `icam-core/src/query.rs` are already this shape: `drop` is a `max` and `slice` a `min`
  over exact per-face fields (`face_sd`) - the global tree, unpruned. Milestone 1: the
  contact query for any line/arc tool against prismatic parts (horizontal faces bounded by
  lines and arcs, vertical walls implied), CPU and GPU, checked against each other, *plus*
  the per-cell pruned tree and its bound field, tested so the certified bound never
  exceeds the exact clearance.
- **B-splines stay native (2026-09-30, Derek).** "Most CAM engines do the lazy thing and
  approximate as lines and arcs"; ours keep every B-spline exact until a post processor
  forces a lower form. `icam-core/src/bspline.rs`: rational curves in homogeneous form,
  Bezier decomposition by The NURBS Book algorithm 5.6 (DecomposeCurve), flat pieces with a stored
  thickness (a certified distance lower bound), branch-and-bound closest point with
  Newton polish, exact curvature and area. The importer keeps STEP B-spline edges and
  accepts vertical B-spline and extrusion walls; the GPU kernel evaluates the same flat
  rational Bezier pieces (chord-minus-thickness bound, Newton foot) and reports the source
  NURBS parameter at every contact, so downstream stages can stay in spline parameters.
- **F-rep compiler, version 1 (2026-09-30), `icam-core/src/frep.rs`.** A prismatic solid on
  its bottom compiles to `min over levels k of max(sd_Sk(x, y), z - z_k, z_bot - z)`: nested
  columns, so no internal seams; each section leaf `sd_Sk` is the exact signed distance to
  the cross-section under level k, whose boundary is the curves of the upward faces at or
  above z_k whose walls reach below it (from the STEP edge-to-face topology). The field is
  1-Lipschitz with its zero set exactly on the boundary, so it is the bound field, exact
  wherever one leaf is active; evaluation reports the active leaf and the source face and
  curve. `prune(box)` drops every min/max child the interval bounds prove inactive in the
  box and is an exact restriction. Multiple solids join by `min`. Overhangs and parts not
  in their machining orientation are refused by name. **Version 2** replaces each section
  leaf with a per-curve CSG subtree over untrimmed primitives (B-rep to CSG conversion with
  separating primitives: Shapiro and Vossler 1993, Dobkin et al. 1993, reference entries
  69-72) without changing anything above the leaves.
- **Exact 3D solids (2026-09-30), `icam-core/src/curve3.rs`, `surface3.rs`, `solid3.rs`.**
  General B-rep solids keep every face on its exact surface: planes, circular cylinders and
  cones, spheres, tori, and rational B-spline surfaces (Bezier patches by the same
  algorithm 5.6, run row by row then column by column); edges are exact lines, arcs,
  ellipses and B-splines trimmed to their vertices. The result is the **exact field** of a
  general solid: signed distance with its nearest point and feature. Each analytic surface
  has one local minimum of distance, so a face contributes its foot when the foot lies in the
  face and its edges otherwise; B-spline faces use a branch and bound over patches, where a
  patch that no edge comes near is wholly in or out of the face and one trim test settles it.
  The sign comes from topology (outward normal at a face foot, the two faces' normals at an
  edge point, the angle-weighted normal at a vertex).
  **Trims are decided on the surface, with no parameter-space curves**: the test
  parts' STEP files carry none. From the foot, walk an exact planar section of the
  surface (a ray on a plane, a generator of a cylinder or cone, a meridian or latitude
  circle, a great circle; two legs on closed surfaces), or an iso-curve of a B-spline surface
  (an exact rational Bezier curve); find the face's edge crossings in closed form, by
  Bernstein root isolation, or by certified curve-curve branch and bound; the first crossing
  decides, since the walk leaves the face there exactly when the edge's in-face direction
  (outward normal cross travel tangent) opposes it. Walks through a vertex or tangent to an
  edge are dropped for the next one. Checks (`icam-check solid`): the signed distance is
  1-Lipschitz on random point pairs, points stepped just inside and just outside every edge
  classify correctly, and on prismatic parts the distance equals the prismatic exact
  distance. On seven test parts the distance had no Lipschitz violations; the trim test
  still misclassifies 0.2-1% of its steps on three of them (left open, see below). Not yet:
  spindle tori, assembly placements. **Periodic B-spline surfaces (2026-10-02):** a surface
  closed on itself in u or v is read. The trim walk along a closed direction wraps around
  the seam: the first crossing after the start still decides, and a full turn with no
  crossing tries another direction. A face with no trim pieces is the whole surface. On
  the test part that needed it: no Lipschitz violations, and all 1,728
  steps into faces and 1,173 steps out of them classify correctly. Open: points inside a
  closed B-spline surface make the trimmed-face search run to its step cap (up to about
  1.3 s for one distance), because many patches lie at nearly the same distance.
  **Role (Derek, 2026-09-30): this is the import-time reference, not the runtime path.**
  *(Rewritten 2026-10-27, Derek: the exact evaluator over certified loops is the field and the
  path-walking trim test described above retires. See the last entry of this section.)*
  Point-in-face walks, seams and tolerance slivers are B-rep evaluation, which the F-rep
  replaces: a point on a primitive's surface is on the part's boundary exactly when F = 0
  there. The layer's job is to tell the compiler which faces cross each cell, and to check
  the compiled F by sampling, as `exact_boundary_distance` checks F-rep version 1.
- **F-rep compiler, version 2 plan: per-cell leaves, no global implicits (2026-09-30).**
  **Reversed 2026-10-02 (Derek): the F-rep must be one global function.** This plan broke

  B-spline faces never get a global implicit (implicitizing a bicubic patch gives degree 18
  with extraneous sheets). Each cell carries only what crosses it: no face (constant sign
  and a distance bound); one face (signed distance to its untrimmed surface, sign from the
  normal, valid while the cell is inside the surface's reach, and with the end patch
  continued polynomially where the patch border falls inside the cell); one edge (max of
  the two faces at a convex edge, min at a concave one, convexity read once from the
  B-rep); one vertex (a small monotone boolean formula over the three or four faces there).
  Refining cells until each holds one face, edge or vertex makes max/min by convexity exact
  in nearly every case, so the global separation problem (Shapiro and Vossler) mostly
  disappears. Max and min keep the field 1-Lipschitz with the right zero set (a bound
  field), exact wherever one face leaf is active; edge cells also carry their exact STEP
  edge curve for the exact field. Order: planes and cylinders (the test nest), then cones,
  spheres and tori, then B-spline faces with patch continuation.
- **F-rep compiler, version 3 proposal: glued local formulas (2026-10-02, Claude, for Derek's
  review; not adopted).** Full statement, proof and measurements in `docs/FREP_GLUING.md`. The
  global CSG route is solved in the literature only for quadric faces (entries 70, 72, 76); the
  algebraic route for B-spline faces fails by its own account (entry 80). frep3's seams came
  from reading only the leaf that holds x, not from its per-cell formulas. Fade each formula to
  zero at the edge of its support and take F = max(0, maxᵢ φ̂ᵢ) + min(0, minᵢ φ̂ᵢ). That is one
  global function, proved 1-Lipschitz, signed like the solid, zero exactly on the boundary,
  never above the distance, and exactly restricted per cell, because valid local formulas
  cannot disagree in sign. The global separation problem is replaced by certifying each local
  formula on its support. On the test nest (harness evaluator, `frep3.rs` unchanged), supports of
  1.25 cell radii give 0 sign errors, 0 overshoots and 1 continuity break of 0.002 mm (a
  probe-chosen formula), against 244 breaks of up to 945 mm today. Tangent-edge selects get an
  always-continuous min/max form with a consensus term.
  **Decision, 2026-10-02 (Derek): the per-cell field routes are dead.** Reading one leaf per
  point and the glued bound field above are both dropped as fields. Seen in slices
  (`icam-check slice`), neither carries anything like the medial-axis information roughing
  needs, and the one-leaf field breaks at cell faces. The field planning needs is the exact
  signed distance; the construction being worked on for it is recorded in
  `docs/FREP_GLUING.md` as a proposal for Derek's review.
- **The exact field: adopted with conditions (2026-10-25, Derek).** The pure field of
  `docs/FREP_GLUING.md` becomes the runtime exact field. F, one CSG formula (the min over the
  octree's cells of max(support ball, cell formula)), gives the sign and decides which surface
  points are on the boundary; E, the distance to the nearest such point among the surfaces'
  critical points, the curves where two surfaces meet and the points where three meet, is the
  field. Conditions: F evaluates as its whole formula, in value and not only in sign; queries read
  compiled data and F only (no vertex, edge curve, face list, loop or `solid3` call); formulas come
  from rules, never from the B-rep's answers (no probes, no certificate checked against the
  reference, no `solid3` signs for empty cells, no point-in-face choice of operator), and a cell no
  rule covers refuses the solid at the depth limit; the gate is the three test parts, the 55 STEP
  Tools AP203 case studies and THE PART against the B-rep reference within file tolerance, with no
  sign errors and no Lipschitz breaks. The tests and guard rules that keep glued, local and B-rep
  evaluation out are in `docs/EXACT_FIELD.md`.
- **The pure field is rejected (2026-10-25, Derek, the same day).** Built under the conditions
  above and judged against the project's four guarantees (fast parallel evaluation of exact
  true fields, no holes, no brittle surface-intersection or B-rep logic), it fails three: E
  finds the curve where two surfaces meet by Newton onto the pair, a slide along the crossing
  and bisection to the exposed part's end (intersection logic with F in place of trim loops),
  decides "on the boundary" by a sign change across an epsilon step, and searches candidate
  pieces per point (34 to 277 points per second against the reference). F stays as the sign and
  zero-set oracle; the distance away from the surface comes from a parallel field solve on F
  (an eikonal redistance from its zero set, on the GPU), never from intersections. Decision 10 in
  `CONTRIBUTING.md`; the verdict and the guard rules are in `docs/EXACT_FIELD.md`.
- **The exact evaluator is the field (2026-10-27, Derek).** Rewrites the 2026-09-29 "compile to
  one global F-rep", the 2026-09-30 "import-time reference, not the runtime path" and decision
  10's ban on a trim test at a query. Behind it: on 2026-10-26 Derek made the STEP file's
  declared tolerance the accuracy line at edges and read guarantee 4 as "no holes or failures in
  the exact inside/outside test, and no edge finding whose search time explodes". The files
  carry no parameter-space curves, so trim loops are built at import: per face, cubic pieces
  fitted to the preimages of the exact edges, certified within the file's tolerance by a
  Bernstein bound, linked by vertex, read by exact crossing parity in a cell tree (always an
  answer). `solid3::Exact` gives the signed distance from them: a face's nearest critical
  point when its loops put it in the face, else its edges and vertices, signed by normals and
  pseudo-normals, with undecided answers marked unsure. The signed distance is one global
  function by definition, so nothing seams; no CSG tree is compiled, since global CSG needs
  separating primitives fitted B-spline faces cannot supply (8,016 tangent edges in the suite,
  2,105 on THE PART). The global field for planning and viewing is the evaluation sampled into
  the sparse brick cache; the eikonal redistance from F is no longer the route. Judged against
  the four guarantees before adoption: guarantee 1 met only with the step-5 GPU kernel (the
  CPU evaluator is a per-point search, 2,030 to 2,850 queries a second on THE PART on all
  threads, against 11 for the old B-rep distance), guarantee 2 met (every check clean on THE
  PART, no unflagged failure in 393,200 suite queries), guarantee 3 met except flagged holes
  (refused loops, cusp edges, capped searches near far poles), guarantee 4 met as Derek reads
  it. CONTRIBUTING.md decisions 1, 2, 3 and 10, the guard (rules.mjs and the prompt hooks) and
  `docs/EXACT_FIELD.md` were rewritten to match. Construction and measurements:
  `docs/SPAN_IMPLICIT.md` sections 12 to 15. Next: the brick cache and a
  one-step view of THE PART, then the GPU kernel, then frep3 and the old trim test are deleted.
  (2026-10-30: frep3 deleted; the old trim test remains.)

### 10. Patent posture (not legal advice)

The live patents near this work (high-efficiency roughing, toolpath linking, 5-axis slot
milling, feature recognition and tool assignment) were read claim by claim on 2026-10-26; the
maintainer keeps that reading. Infringement is read claim by claim against what the program
does; intent and how a path was derived do not enter, so a field method avoids a claim only by
missing an element of it. The rules that follow from the reading:

- **Toolpaths** (decision 8 in `CONTRIBUTING.md`): no maximum and minimum engagement angle is
  chosen for a path to vary between; chip thickness is the load measure, with feed free. No
  region is split into a spiral part and a trochoid-only remainder. Links run only through
  space already empty, and no region is cut, or ordered early, to host links. No Voronoi
  diagram of boundary segments; spirals come from solved fields. In 5-axis, flank positions
  never come from a slot's two walls, and until May 2032, no simultaneous 5-axis loops whose
  upper and lower patterns differ while stepping down a slot between two walls.
- **Trochoids and field spirals are allowed** (Derek, 2026-10-26). The engagement-limiting
  patents on circular passes have expired, and trochoidal milling predates them. Field spirals
  rest on Bieterman and Sandstrom (2003), PDE spirals whose stepover varies along each loop.
- **Feature recognition** (Derek, 2026-10-25): regions are found in the field after import,
  where the removal, reach A(x, d), fit and floor fields agree, with no B-rep feature
  templates; what a region means (a thread, a tolerance) comes from PMI. Regions come only from
  field values, and nothing groups faces or F-rep leaves by shared edges, in planning or in the
  UI. PMI is sampled into the field from the faces it names, never grouped by walking their
  edges. The import compiler reads edge convexity (decision 4) but never groups faces into
  features, finds a perimeter or highlights anything.
- **Tool assignment:** plan each tool's toolpaths from the stock the earlier tools' actual
  paths left, not in parallel from shares found by subtracting reach. Prior art: Yao, Gupta and
  Nau (2003, Computer-Aided Design 35(9)) compute the area each cutter can cut and search
  cutter sequences for the least time; Veeramani and Gau (2000, IIE Transactions 32(7)) choose
  multiple tool sizes for 2.5D pockets.
- **Open:** patent-database searches beyond the engines read so far. Run them before any
  public "patent-free" statement.
- **Strategy:** publishing openly *creates prior art*, pre-empting future fences around
  implicit CAM toolpaths. This is the highest-leverage move and aligns with the open ethos.

### Cross-cutting abstractions that MUST exist from day one (the 5-axis through-lines)

These are designed in from Phase 1 even though 2.5D does not exercise them fully, so that
5-axis is a *generalization*, not a rewrite. **Each is stated in its 5-axis general form,
with the 3-axis/2.5D restriction called out as the MVP implementation:** the general form is
the architectural contract; the restriction is only what Phase 1 actually builds. The point
is not to build 5-axis early — it is to avoid writing a commitment in a shape that is
secretly the low-dimensional case.

1. **Tool is always an SDF assembly** `T(x; pose)` = flute ∪ shank ∪ holder, with the
   *cutting* portion tagged separately from the *non-cutting* portion. 2.5D uses only the
   flute profile; 5-axis collision needs shank/holder. Never model a tool as "just a
   radius."
2. **Pose: store a frame, but optimize and interpolate on `ℝ³ × S²`.** A pose is
   `(position c, tool-axis â)` for the positioning problem; a path is a sequence of these. A
   milling tool is a surface of revolution, so roll about `â` has no effect on material
   removal or (symmetric-holder) collision — the positioning manifold is **5-DOF: `ℝ³ × S²`,
   not 6-DOF on `SO(3)`**. A full frame may be *stored* (feed-direction reference, asymmetric
   tooling), but the pose optimizer and the tool-axis interpolator operate on `ℝ³ × S²`:
   interpolate `â` on `S²` (not the frame on `SO(3)`) for a C¹ axis trajectory, so a phantom
   roll cannot inject spurious non-smoothness into the axis-smoothness term.
   *3-axis restriction (MVP):* `â` pinned to `(0,0,1)`; 5-axis unpins it onto `S²`. Never
   hardcode Z-only positions — and never carry roll as an optimization DOF.
3. **Engagement and collision are always field queries around the posed tool.** The same
   sampler serves 2.5D engagement and 5-axis engagement + shank/holder collision. Build it
   pose-general.
4. **Toolpath generation = trajectory integration + a constrained solve, with tool-axis
   assignment behind its own interface.** The integrator carries a growable *state vector*
   (2.5D: `(position, pitch)`; 5-axis: `(position, tool-axis, pitch)`). A per-step *local*
   solve is well-suited to scalar/engagement feedback (2.5D pitch, 3-axis engagement). But
   **tool-axis selection is NOT assumed local**: the feasible axis set is non-convex and
   position-dependent, and a locally-optimal axis can force a discontinuous flip downstream,
   so real axis planning is often *global* on the orientation bundle (sample the admissible
   cone per station → graph → smooth min-cost axis trajectory). The integrator therefore
   consumes an **axis-assignment interface** — "produce a feasible, smooth,
   kinematically-valid `â(s)` over the path" — implementable *either* locally (per-step
   descent) *or* globally (path-level planning), without the integrator's contract presuming
   locality. *3-axis restriction (MVP):* the axis interface returns the constant `(0,0,1)`
   and the only live local solve is engagement feedback. Do **not** shape the integrator's
   orientation slot as a per-step hook.
5. **Differentiability everywhere.** Analytic gradients feed the roughing direction field
   now and the 5-axis pose gradient descent later. (A reason Fidget is chosen.)
6. **The removable-region field `R` is dimension-independent.** Per-slab in 2.5D, volumetric
   in 3D/5-axis. Same definition.
7. **Two-field discipline** (conservative bound + exact band) is in the type system from the
   start, even though roughing mostly uses the conservative field.
8. **Machine kinematics is a cross-cutting *service*, not a post-only step.** The machine
   model (forward/inverse kinematics, joint limits, singularity proximity, joint-velocity
   caps) must be *queryable from Layers 4/5* during pose selection and feed scheduling: a
   geometrically-valid pose can be kinematically unreachable, near-singular, or demand
   impossible rotary speed, and that must inform *generation*, not just posting. Layer 6 still
   owns final linearization/posting, but the *model* is a shared interface consulted upstream.
   *3-axis restriction (MVP):* the kinematics interface is the identity map (XYZ = TCP, no
   rotary, no singularities) — it exists and is consulted, but answers trivially. Never make
   5-axis kinematics reachable *only* from the post.

## Part III — Phased Roadmap

Each phase ends with a **verifiable artifact** (a machined-simulatable G-code result and a
measurable property). Each phase is a strict capability superset of the previous one.

**Phases 1 + 2 (true 3-axis rough + finish) are the adoption floor** — the minimum that earns
real-world use. 2.5D-only has ~zero standalone value (every free CAM does it), so it is a
Phase-0 spike, not a milestone. 5-axis (Phases 3–4) is the intended endpoint, built as a
generalization of the floor, not a rewrite of it.

### Phase 0 — Foundation spike
**Goal:** de-risk the kernel, lock the core abstractions, and prove the walk loop in its
trivial (2.5D) form.

- Integrate Fidget; wrap point/interval/gradient eval behind `icam-field`.
- Implement the *through-line* types: `ToolAssembly` (SDF, cutting/non-cutting tags), `Pose`
  (frame storage; optimize/interpolate on `ℝ³ × S²`), two-field wrapper (conservative +
  exact-band handle), path = `Vec<Pose>`. Stub the **kinematics interface** (identity map)
  and the **axis-assignment interface** (returns `(0,0,1)`) — so their call sites exist
  before 5-axis fills them (through-lines 4, 8).
- **Throughput + interval-tightness gate:** define a representative CSG part; benchmark
  point/interval/gradient eval at CAM-scale query volumes (millions–billions), *and* measure
  interval **tightness** on a deep tree — the dependency problem inflates interval bounds
  with depth and degrades pruning, and both the gouge-free guarantee and the drop-cutter
  root-find need *tight* intervals, not merely fast ones. *Gate:* is analytic eval fast *and
  tight* enough that we never voxelize the part? **Pre-decide the fallback if it fails** (a
  high-res narrow-band *query cache* for the part, analytic tree retained for finishing; or
  GPU eval) rather than discovering it at the gate.
- **Error-budget experiment (pointed at the δ-band):** compare raw `min`/`max` field vs a
  redistanced ground truth **specifically in the finish-stock δ-band at concave features and
  near the medial axis** (§7 caveat) — that is the error that actually reaches the finisher,
  not generic offset error.
- **2.5D walk spike:** run the field-walking rougher (§8) in the trivial horizontal-slab
  foliation on a prismatic pocket — enough to validate the walker + engagement loop +
  coverage re-seed before Phase 1 makes them freeform. **This is a spike, not a deliverable
  milestone.**
- Minimal `icam-viz` (raymarch the field) and a G-code emitter stub.

**Exit:** eval throughput *and interval tightness* characterized (fallback chosen if gated
out); core types + kinematics/axis-assignment interfaces stable; δ-band error budget
quantified; walk loop demonstrated in the trivial 2.5D case.

### Phase 1 — True 3-axis field-walking rougher  ← ADOPTION FLOOR (with Phase 2)
**Goal:** high-efficiency constant-engagement roughing of **freeform 3D parts**, not just
prismatic 2.5D, by walking the field (§7–§8). The bar is to match a commercial
adaptive-clearing rougher on a sculpted part; 2.5D pockets are the trivial special case
cleared in the Phase-0 spike. The 2.5D→3D jump is small here: same `R`, same walker, same
coverage loop — only `P` becomes a genuine freeform field and the leaf sections become curved
and Z-varying (§8 foliation).

- `icam-stock`: tri-dexel stock with incremental swept-volume subtraction.
- `icam-query`:
  - **engagement sampler generalized to 3D contact** — the engaged region of the flute
    against live stock when a slab bottom meets a *sloped* surface (partial, varying axial
    engagement), not merely the arc in a slab plane. A distinct generalization from
    pose-general (through-line 3); build it explicitly.
  - **gouge-free drop as a tool-*volume* query** — lower the posed tool SDF until the tool
    *volume* first touches the part field (interval arithmetic over the tool), handling
    multi-point contact on saddles/ridges. A single center-ray drop gouges; this corrects the
    §5 Layer 3 "distance along a ray" phrasing.
  - wall-distance for the inward walk term.
- `icam-mat`: per-leaf removable-region seeds/spine (grid skeleton first; OpenVoronoi later).
  The inward walk term *and* the seeds depend on this distance/ridge computation, whose
  discretized gradient is noisiest on the very medial spine it targets — a real Phase-1
  dependency, not a Phase-2 one.
- `icam-motion`: RK4 integrator over state `(position, pitch)`; engagement PID; trochoid
  injection on engagement spike; curvature/jerk limiting; **feed scheduling (constant
  chip-load + HSM corner/entry feed limiting)** — constant engagement without constant chip
  load is not the productivity win, so this rides here, not in Phase 5. Decide whether the
  adaptive stepper or the engagement servo owns step length (avoid coupled-loop oscillation).
- `icam-strategy`:
  - the field-walking rougher — `R = max(S, δ−P)`, leaf-walk spiral (horizontal-slab
    foliation), coverage re-seeding via `{R<0}` residue, multi-component handling, entry via
    helical ramp / plunge / pre-drill.
  - **multi-tool rest-roughing for free** — the residual `{R<0}` after tool 1 *is* the stock
    for tool 2; a big→small cascade is just re-running with updated `S`. A field strength
    offset CAM lacks; make it first-class.
  - **shallow-region / terrace pass** — constant-Zc HEM leaves terraces on near-flat areas;
    add a complementary shallow pass (3D-offset semi-finish or local finer stepdown) so the
    stock handed to finishing is machinable everywhere.
- `icam-post`: linking/retracts; 3-axis G-code with **tolerance-bounded arc/line fitting** of
  the emergent spiral ("no curves constructed" holds only internally; output needs controlled
  chord error).

**Exit / acceptance:** roughing G-code for a **freeform multi-surface part** (multi-pocket
2.5D as a subset), **measured radial engagement within tolerance**, full coverage (no slivers,
no un-cleaned terraces), a big→small multi-tool cascade, a real feed schedule, and smooth HSM
motion — verified in the dexel sim **and cross-checked against an independent MRR reference**
(the dexel drove the controller, so it alone is a consistency check, not verification).

### Phase 2 — True 3-axis surface finishing (redistancing becomes mandatory)  ← ADOPTION FLOOR (with Phase 1)
**Goal:** gouge-free 3-axis surface finishing with controlled scallop. Because
scallop-controlled finishing reads the *metric* (the CL-surface is a true tool-radius
offset), **redistancing is now a critical-path must-have, not a "how much do we need"
experiment.**

- **Drop-cutter finishing first (grid-free, no redistancing):** directed-distance drop-cutter
  (the tool-*volume* root-find from Phase 1) gives the workhorse patterns — raster/parallel,
  radial, spiral, 3D-offset, flowline — plus **waterline/Z-level** for steep walls. Fastest
  route to credible surface machining; do not gate *any* finishing behind redistancing.
- **Steep/shallow split (free from the field):** classify by local slope = Z-component of
  `∇P/|∇P|`; waterline on steep, drop-cutter raster/3D-offset on shallow. Neither pattern
  alone gives good scallop everywhere.
- `icam-redistance`: narrow-band Fast Sweeping (OpenVDB or native) + closest-point projector;
  medial-axis detection doubling as accuracy guard **and** "tool-too-big / feature-unreachable"
  detector — the metric fails exactly where the offset self-intersects.
- **Constant-scallop eikonal finisher (the differentiated follow-on):** solve
  `|∇_S g| = 1/s(x)` on the CL-surface `f=R` (stepover `s` from local curvature via the field
  Hessian); toolpaths = integer contours of `g`. Subsumes the steep/shallow split with
  genuinely constant scallop. First implicit-native finishing strategy.
- **Pencil/rest** finishing via offset-field ridge extraction — inherits the 3D-MAT ridge
  instability parked in Appendix A; treat as lower-confidence until stabilized.

**Exit:** finished surfaces with **measured constant scallop height** and provably gouge-free
contact (tool-volume interval check), full rough→finish pipeline for true 3-axis freeform
parts, cross-checked against an independent verifier. **Phases 1 + 2 together are the adoption
floor.**

### Phase 3 — 3+2 positional 5-axis (the bridge)
**Goal:** run the existing rougher and finishers in an arbitrary *fixed* tool frame; prove
the pose-general and tool-assembly abstractions.

- Generalize all Layer 3 queries to arbitrary fixed orientation (they were built pose-general in
  Phase 1 — this phase *exercises* that).
- **Full tool-assembly collision** (`flute ∪ shank ∪ holder` vs part & fixtures) at tilted
  orientation — the first real use of the non-cutting tool geometry.
- Automatic/assisted orientation selection to reach undercuts; fixture/clamp fields.
- `icam-post`: 3+2 output with tool-orientation setup.

**Exit:** rough+finish an undercut feature at a fixed tilt, collision-checked against the
full holder, posted as 3+2.

### Phase 4 — Simultaneous 5-axis  ← THE INTENDED TARGET
**Goal:** orientation DOFs become *variable and optimized per step*; this is where the
differentiable field pays off categorically.

- `icam-motion` state grows to `(position, orientation, pitch)`; the per-step local solve
  becomes **differentiable gouge-free pose optimization**: gradient-descend orientation so
  the cutting portion stays tangent and the non-cutting portion stays clear, with
  **axis-smoothness**, **machine-limit**, and **singularity-avoidance** terms. Warm-started
  along the path for C¹ orientation.
- **5-axis field-walking roughing** — the §8 spiral generalized to a full 3D flow with
  simultaneous orientation control and volumetric stock (VDB narrow band). This is the
  flagship generalization and is developed in full below (**Phase 4.1**): one constrained
  functional of which 3-axis and 3+2 roughing are frozen-DOF restrictions.
- **Flank / swarf milling** — fit a line-swept tool SDF to a band of the part zero-set
  (optimization against the field).
- `icam-stock` → volumetric narrow-band SDF (VDB) for true 3D swept subtraction.
- `icam-kine`: full 5-axis machine model (IK, joint limits, singularity, velocity), consulted
  by pose selection and feed scheduling upstream (through-line 8); `icam-post` consumes it for
  non-linear posting/linearization and singularity handling.

#### Phase 4.1 — 5-axis field-walking roughing: one functional, no modes

Simultaneous-5-axis *roughing* is a near-empty square in the commercial grid (Part V · Advantage 2).
Shops rough with 3+2 — index, rough as 3-axis, re-index — because true simultaneous roughing
requires collision-aware engagement control against a volumetric in-process stock *while the
tool axis tilts*, and offset/combinatorial CAM cannot represent that. This design can, because
roughing here is one constrained optimization over the tool-configuration trajectory
`q(s) = (c(s), â(s)) ∈ ℝ³ × S²`:

```
minimize   T[q]         # machining time  ≈  maximize ∫ MRR ds,  MRR = engagement·feed·depth
  over     q(s) = (c(s), â(s)) ∈ ℝ³ × S²
subject to
  coverage    sweep{ T_cut(x; q(s)) } ⊇ {R < 0}          # everything removable is removed
  collision   T_noncut(x; q) ∩ (P ∪ S ∪ fixtures) = ∅    # shank/holder/fixture clearance
  engagement  θ_measured(s) ≤ θ_target                   # sampled around the posed cutter — measured, not inferred
  kinematics  â(s) reachable · joint limits · |dâ/ds| ≤ κ · singularity-clear
  finish      (already encoded by  R = max(S, δ − P) )
```

Every term is a field query with an analytic gradient — which is *why this is writable as one
problem here and not elsewhere*.

**The frozen-DOF ladder.** The commercial landscape is not three techniques; it is this one
functional with DOFs frozen or quantized:

| Mode | Axis field `â` over `{R<0}` | Foliation of `{R<0}` | Restriction |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3-axis rough | constant `(0,0,1)` | one flat horizontal stack | axis frozen |
| 3+2 rough | piecewise-constant, `k` human-chosen values | `k` flat stacks, unioned | axis quantized to a finite set |
| Simultaneous 5-axis | continuous `â(s)` on `S²` | one smoothly-curving leaf-stack | none — full functional |

The axis field goes **constant → piecewise-constant → continuous**; 3-axis and 3+2 are the
"`â` locally constant" degeneracies of the §8 leaf-walk (horizontal slabs are the trivial
foliation, axis = slab normal).

**3+2 is a representation artifact, not a machining preference.** Offset roughing must construct
offset curves of a planar region boundary and infer engagement from offset spacing; that needs a
*fixed slicing frame*, which needs the axis held constant over the region. The combinatorial
representation therefore *cannot express a continuously-varying admissible-axis field* — it is
forced to quantize the axis to a few human-picked values. The quantization leaves two artifacts
the continuum does not have:
- **seams** — two flat foliations with different normals do not align at their boundary, so 3+2
  leaves rest-material steps between indexed regions; one continuous leaf-stack has no seams;
- **reachability cliffs** — each index either reaches a feature or does not, with no way to lean
  in progressively as a cavity deepens; continuous `â(s)` keeps a short, stiff tool reaching by
  tilting as it descends.

**The field machines the superset with the same code.** `R = max(S, δ−P)` is pose- and
dimension-independent (through-line 6); engagement is *measured* around the posed cutter and so
is axis-invariant (§7–§8); coverage is `{R<0}`-residue re-seeding and so is topology-agnostic
(Part V · Advantage 7). `R`, the engagement servo, the coverage loop, and the stock model are therefore
identical from 3-axis to 5-axis. **Only the foliation and the axis-coupling generalize** —
horizontal slabs (normal `(0,0,1)`) → part-adapted curved leaves (axis slaved to, or optimized
around, the leaf normal).

**The result: mode selection disappears.** Because the rougher optimizes the functional rather
than selecting a mode, it emits whatever axis field the constraints and objective imply —
constant over rigid open regions, gently curving where a feature deepens, sharply tilted only
where reachability demands — as *one* output. The axis is *as constant as it can be and as
variable as it must be*: where 3-axis is optimal the optimizer chooses a near-constant axis on
its own (3+2's rigidity, without the seams), and it spends axis motion only where that motion
buys reachability. "3-axis vs 3+2 vs simultaneous" ceases to be a mode the user picks and becomes
a description of what different regions of one output happened to look like.

**Open (Phase 4) — the research core.** The field framing makes this *well-posed and
differentiable*, the precondition combinatorial CAM cannot reach; it does not make it free. Two
genuine difficulties are the center of the phase, promoted here from the §8 foliation aside:
1. **Reachability-constrained smooth foliation with coverage.** In 3-axis the foliation is free
   (horizontal planes always work). In 5-axis the curved leaves must be chosen so each is
   reachable by a feasible collision-free axis (Part V · Advantage 3), the slaved axis field is smooth
   enough for the rotaries (`|dâ/ds| ≤ κ`), *and* the leaves still tile `{R<0}` completely. These
   three pull against one another; every ingredient is a differentiable field query, but the
   choice of foliation is genuinely unsolved in general.
2. **State-coupled sequencing.** Reachability depends on how much stock is left, which depends on
   the order the leaves are machined — a combinatorial sequencing problem coupled to the removal
   *state*, worse than the static axis non-convexity of through-line 4. The redeeming structure:
   the state is itself the field `S`, evolving by the same swept subtraction, so the search
   operates on a queryable field rather than a bespoke IPW model.

Two dependencies close the loop: verification of coverage + collision-freedom over the
*continuous* rough motion rests on the interval-over-time machinery (Part V · Advantage 6), not
per-station checks; and rigidity/overhang/predictability — the reasons a machinist *prefers* a
steady axis — must enter the objective as an axis-motion / overhang penalty, or the optimizer
will produce geometrically clever but mechanically fragile motion.

**Exit:** simultaneous 5-axis rough+finish of a curved/undercut part, collision- and
gouge-free by construction, with smooth optimized tool-axis motion, posted for a real
5-axis kinematic configuration.

## Part IV — How each phase builds toward 5-axis (through-line map)

| Abstraction | Phase 1 (2.5D) | Phase 4 (5-axis) | Continuity |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tool model | flute profile of `T(x;pose)` | full `flute∪shank∪holder` collision | Same SDF assembly; more of it used |
| Pose | axis pinned to `(0,0,1)` | axis free on `S²`, optimized | Same `ℝ³×S²` manifold (frame stored, roll never optimized); axis unpinned |
| Integrator state | `(position, pitch)` | `(position, tool-axis, pitch)` | Same RK4 skeleton; state vector grows |
| Axis assignment | interface returns `(0,0,1)` | local *or* global feasible/smooth `â(s)` | Same axis-assignment interface; not a per-step hook |
| Per-step local solve | engagement PID | engagement + feed scheduling | Same local slot; scalar feedback only (axis is NOT here) |
| Removable region `R` | per-slab | volumetric | Same definition, higher dimension |
| Walk foliation | horizontal slabs | part-adapted surfaces | Same leaf-walk; foliation generalizes, slab is the trivial case |
| Stock | tri-dexel | VDB narrow band | Same subtract/query interface |
| Queries (Layer 3) | pose-general, used at Z-only | pose-general, used at any axis | Written general once; exercised more |
| Kinematics | identity (XYZ = TCP) | IK/limits/singularity queried in-loop | Same interface; identity→full, consulted from Layers 4/5 |
| Differentiability | direction field for walk | pose gradient descent | Same analytic gradients (Fidget) |

**The design invariant:** nothing about 2.5D is allowed to hardcode an assumption that
breaks in 5-axis. The rougher, the queries, the tool, and the integrator are all built
pose-general and field-native from Phase 1; the later phases *unpin DOFs and add terms*,
they do not replace subsystems.

## Part V — Categorical advantages in the 5-axis solution space

The Euclidean crux (§5) forces this project to be disciplined: analytic fields, exact gradients,
interval certification, an explicitly discretized stock. The thesis of this section is that
*those same properties are what dissolve the problems mesh- and offset-based 5-axis CAM find
intractable*. The defensive discipline and the offensive advantage are the same coin. Each item
states the problem conventional CAM struggles with, why the field formulation removes it, and —
in the register of the §7 δ-band caveat — the honest catch or dependency, so the claim survives
an adversarial read. Advantages 1–3 are the headline claims; Advantages 4–6 are differentiated strategies that
follow from them; Advantages 7–9 are sharper statements of advantages already present in the design.

### Advantage 1 — Grazing-contact accuracy is best exactly where 5-axis lives

Mesh/tessellation CAM checks gouge and contact against a *faceted* part, and chordal error is
not uniform in its consequences: at **near-tangent (grazing) incidence a small facet-normal error
produces a large contact-point error**, because the contact point slides along the near-flat
approach. Grazing contact *is* the 5-axis regime — high lead/lean, near-flank contact,
curvature-matched passes — so mesh CAM is least accurate exactly where 5-axis is most aggressive
("the sim was clean but the tool gouged" is the folk symptom). Interval-evaluated analytic fields
(§9, Layer 1) carry no facets, so the gouge-free guarantee is exact *independent of incidence angle* —
the grazing regime is no different from any other. **The advantage of analytic-over-mesh is not
uniform; it is concentrated in the grazing-contact regime that defines 5-axis machining.** This
turns "we are more accurate" (every vendor's claim) into a specific, geometry-backed claim mesh
CAM cannot structurally match, and it is the concrete justification for staying implicit-native
rather than caching to a mesh (§4).

**Caveat —** the guarantee is only as tight as the interval bounds; the Phase-0 interval-tightness
gate is what makes this real rather than asymptotic.

### Advantage 2 — Simultaneous 5-axis roughing is a near-empty square in the commercial grid

Commercial CAM roughs with 3+2 (index, rough as 3-axis, re-index) because true simultaneous
roughing needs collision-aware engagement control against a volumetric in-process stock *while
the axis tilts* — which offset methods cannot represent. This architecture has the three
ingredients nobody has assembled: a **volumetric stock field** (VDB narrow band), an engagement
controller servoing on **measured** (not inferred) engagement, and a **differentiable pose**.
Reachability is what forces simultaneous motion (deep cavities, undercuts, ports, blisk/impeller
hubs), and the general method *degenerates to 3-axis/3+2 automatically where those are optimal* —
so this is not a niche capability bolted on, it is the superset of which today's roughing modes
are frozen-DOF restrictions. It is arguably the flagship result of the project — the emptiest
square in the grid — and is developed in full in **Phase 4.1**.

### Advantage 3 — Accessibility/collision is a differentiable field over ℝ³×S², not a combinatorial visibility problem

The classical 5-axis accessibility problem — which tool orientations are collision-free at each
cutter-contact point — is the **visibility/accessibility-cone** problem, attacked by discretely
sampling orientations and ray-casting or mapping configuration space: combinatorially expensive,
resolution-limited, and the discreteness is itself a source of jerky axis motion (the planner
hops between sampled admissible orientations). Here collision is a field inequality
`T(x;pose) ∩ (P ∪ S ∪ fixtures) = ∅`, and **penetration depth and its gradient with respect to
pose are analytically available** — so the admissible-orientation set is a semi-algebraic set
with computable boundary and gradients, and accessibility becomes a smooth constraint one can run
continuation/homotopy on along the path.

**Caveat —** through-line 4 already states the honest limit: this set is **non-convex and can be
disconnected**, so a locally-optimal axis can force a downstream discontinuous flip; real axis
planning is *global* (admissible cone per station → graph → min-cost smooth trajectory). The
field framing still pays — each node's feasibility and each edge's cost are analytic field
queries with gradients, not re-runs of a discrete collision engine. The claim is not "gradient
descent solves it" but "the visibility-cone problem — historically among the hardest combinatorial
problems in multi-axis planning — becomes a smooth constrained problem on the orientation bundle."

### Advantage 4 — Analytic differential geometry as a shared service (normals, curvature, shape operator)

The whole local differential geometry of the part is **analytic and exact**: the normal is
`∇P/|∇P|`, and the shape operator — principal curvatures and directions — is the eigenstructure
of the field Hessian `∇²P`. Mesh CAM estimates curvature by finite differences on facets, which
is noisy and *worst at high curvature*, precisely where it matters most. Exact differential
geometry is a shared service consumed all over the stack: curvature-adaptive scallop/stepover
(the Phase-2 eikonal finisher), steep/shallow and feature classification, the tangency terms of
the pose optimizer, and — as *one* consumer among these — curvature-matched machining with
barrel/circle-segment tooling where a shop uses it. Framing it as a service rather than a single
strategy is deliberate: the win is the exact local geometry, not any one tool style built on it.

**Caveat —** the Hessian of a raw `min`/`max` CSG field is discontinuous at feature edges and
unreliable near the medial axis (§5). Any curvature-driven strategy must run on the *exact-band*
field, not the conservative one — a further justification for the Phase-2 redistancing investment,
not a free consequence of the raw field.

### Advantage 5 — Flank/swarf generalizes to envelope milling: fit any tool's swept envelope to a surface band

Flank/swarf milling of ruled surfaces is among the hardest 5-axis problems; for **non-developable**
surfaces the error is irreducible, the state of the art is heavy motion optimization, and it is
almost always restricted to *cylindrical* tools. Two generalizations fall out of the field
formulation. (1) The objective is clean: machining error is the integral of `P` over the tool's
contact band — differentiable in the tool's motion parameters — and interval arithmetic yields a
**certified worst-case error bound over the whole band**, not a sampled RMS. (2) Because the tool
is an arbitrary SDF, this is **not restricted to cylindrical flank milling**: you fit the swept
envelope of *any* tool (barrel, conical, tapered). "Flank milling" thus generalizes to **envelope
milling: fit any tool's swept envelope to any surface strip, with a certified error bound.**

**Caveat —** for non-developable targets the residual has a nonzero floor; you cannot drive it to
zero. The deliverable is the *provably minimum-error* fit plus a certified bound — which also
decides, per band, whether flank milling is admissible for the tolerance at all.

### Advantage 6 — Exact swept envelope and *dynamic* (between-station) gouge-freedom via interval-over-time

Two classical pains. (a) The exact swept volume of a moving tool — the envelope of a two-parameter
family of surfaces, with grazing curves — is hard, and it is what both stock update and true gouge
checking need. (b) **Dynamic gouging:** conventional CAM checks gouge at discrete cutter-contact
points and can miss a gouge that occurs *between* them as the axis interpolates — a notorious
5-axis failure. Here the swept volume is `min over t of T(x; pose(t))` (an infimal convolution
along the trajectory) and the grazing condition is the clean analytic `∂ₜ T(x; pose(t)) = 0`.
Better: **interval arithmetic over the time parameter** — interval-bounding `pose(t)` across a
whole motion segment — yields a *certified* gouge-free guarantee for the **continuous motion
between stations**, not merely at sampled points. This is the motion-level analog of the static
interval gouge guarantee (Advantage 1), and it is a load-bearing dependency for Phase 4.1 verification, not
a nice-to-have. The doc currently treats swept-volume subtraction only as stock bookkeeping
(§6, §9); the *query* side stated here is a distinct correctness claim.

### Advantages 7–9 — Sharper statements of advantages already in the design

Three where the mechanism is present but the claim is undersold:

- **Advantage 7 — Topology-change robustness (from §8).** As stock is removed, `{R<0}` changes topology —
  pockets merge, islands appear and vanish, a region splits. Combinatorial CAM must re-detect
  regions and manage island appearance/disappearance; it is a fragility and missed-material
  source. The coverage loop (query connected components of `{R<0}`, re-seed residue) is
  **topology-agnostic** — the walker does not know or care how many components exist or how they
  merged. §8 states the mechanism; the payoff is that it *dissolves the island/topology-management
  problem*, uniformly across 3-axis, 3+2, and 5-axis.

- **Advantage 8 — One representation across design and manufacture (bigger than the Phase-5 SIREN bet).**
  Because process targets are fields, scallop / engagement / stock-to-leave can be
  **spatially-varying** — finer scallop where the shape operator (Advantage 4) says curvature is high, more
  finish stock near thin walls (part medial axis = wall-thickness field), heavier engagement where
  structure allows. Deeper: a design-authored *importance/tolerance field* (the implicit /
  lattice / gradient design workflow) can drive the toolpath **directly, with no lossy handoff
  through B-rep/STL** — design and manufacturing plan live in one representation. This is the
  field-native version of the "single differentiable design→toolpath pipeline" that Phase 5
  gestures at via SIREN, available *without* neural fields the moment strategies read
  spatially-varying targets. It belongs as a consequence of the architecture, not only a research
  bullet.

- **Advantage 9 — Machine-space folded into geometric generation (sharpening through-line 8).** Because
  generation is differentiable *and* the machine model is queryable in-loop (through-line 8),
  singularity proximity, joint-limit margin, and rotary-velocity cost are **soft-constraint terms
  in the same objective that selects the tool axis** — one unified optimization over
  `ℝ³ × S² × machine-config`. The conventional flow is generate-geometry → check-kinematics →
  hack-retracts; here machine limits are *inputs to* axis selection, optimized jointly with the
  geometry. The pieces are in Phase 4; the sharper claim is the *joint* optimization, not a
  post-hoc check.

**The through-line of Part V:** the discipline the crux forces — analytic, differentiable,
interval-certified, field-native state — is exactly the machinery that makes the intractable
5-axis problems *well-posed*. The honesty and the ambition are one coin. Note the dependencies so
no claim is read as free: Advantage 4 and Advantage 8's curvature-driven scallop ride on the exact-band field
(§5, Phase 2); Advantage 2 and Advantage 6 ride on interval-over-time verification; Advantage 3 rides on the global,
non-convex axis solve of through-line 4.
